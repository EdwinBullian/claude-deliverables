Weekly market recap, week of Oct 19

The S&P 500 fell 1.0% this week to 7,624, and the Nasdaq fell 1.7% to 25,955. The Dow rose 0.5% to 52,215. When the Dow rises while the Nasdaq falls, the weakness is concentrated in the kinds of companies the Nasdaq holds more of. This week that meant technology and communication services, which were two of the three worst sectors. For Eddie, the week was mostly about which of his names sit in those sectors.

Communication services was the worst sector at -4.3%, followed by industrials at -2.2% and technology at -2.0%. The best sectors were energy at +2.0%, health care at +2.0% and consumer staples at +1.6%. Energy, health care and staples are generally the parts of the market that move least with the economy or with tech sentiment. They led while the growth sectors lagged. That pattern means money was moving toward steadier businesses, though the week's data does not say why.

The most-covered name was SpaceX (SPCX), with 76 stories. It reported its first earnings as a public company, and the coverage was strongly positive. Revenue rose 161%, and one headline said analysts' estimates were off by $1.6 billion. Another reported that SpaceX swung to a profit. The stock rose between roughly 6.8% and 11.2% on different days. The percentages differ because each article measured at a different point in the trading day. One earlier update showed the stock down 3.0% while the report was still being awaited.

The second SpaceX story was a merger. Elon Musk said on the earnings call that a SpaceX-Tesla merger is under consideration, that it "would be a good idea," and that he sees "a lot of merit" in it. Separate reports said the two companies had formed an exploration committee that excludes Musk, and that the boards were studying a combination that could be worth $3.5 trillion. One headline noted that a combined company would hold about 30,000 bitcoin. These are reports of exploration, not an agreed deal. Nothing in the coverage says terms exist or that the boards have approved anything.

Tesla (TSLA) is on Eddie's watchlist and rose 4.1% for the week to $372.22. The coverage put its gains at about 3.4% to 3.7% on the days the merger reports came out. Tesla's own earnings report is still pending. That makes the merger story harder to read, because the stock is now reacting to two things at once: the possible deal and the coming quarterly numbers. A rise on merger talk can fade or reverse if the talk goes nowhere, so Tesla's move this week reflects expectations about a deal that has not happened.

A third SpaceX item was a lockup expiry. About 2.3 billion SpaceX shares were reported to be leaving their lockup period. A lockup is a set time after an IPO during which early investors and insiders are not allowed to sell. When it ends, those holders can sell, and the extra supply can push a price down if many of them do. SpaceX stock was up 8.8% on the day this was covered, so the market was not treating the expiry as a problem at that point. Whether selling follows later is something to watch.

Meta (META) was the most-covered stock on Eddie's watchlist after SpaceX, with 56 stories. A judge ordered it to pay New Mexico $25 billion over violations of state law tied to the Cambridge Analytica breach. The stock was up about 1.3% to 1.4% on the day the ruling was reported, which suggests the market did not treat it as a large threat to the company's finances at that moment. For the full week, though, Meta fell 9.1% to $642.62. It is not clear what drove the full-week decline. The penalty landed close to Meta's third-quarter earnings, which adds to the uncertainty about how investors are judging the stock.

Alphabet (GOOGL) fell 6.8% to $320.21. Meta and Alphabet are both classified in communication services, the sector that fell 4.3%, so their declines fit the sector's weakness. The week's information does not give a company-specific reason for Alphabet's drop. Sector-wide selling is often a large part of why a stock falls in a given week, and a stock can decline with its group even without bad news of its own.

The other technology names on the list also fell. AMD dropped 6.3% to $516.79 and Nvidia (NVDA) lost 3.3% to $210.96. Intel (INTC) fell 8.0% to $86.96 and Dell fell 3.0% to $456.44. All four are in or closely tied to technology, which fell 2.0% as a sector. Nvidia had 33 stories, more than any of these others, but the week's coverage does not tie its decline to a specific event. The declines in AMD and Intel were larger than the sector's, which means those two fell more than the average tech stock.

The biggest losses came from the two names with the least explanation. Bloom Energy (BE) fell 17.1% to $193.30, with 25 stories written about it. GE Vernova (GEV), which Eddie holds, fell 12.9% to $815.00. A drop of that size in a holding deserves attention, even though no single cause for it is established here. Both companies are tied to power and electrical equipment, so their weakness may be related. Industrials fell 2.2% on the week, which probably contributed to GEV's slide, but that does not account for most of a 12.9% decline.

Eddie's other holding, Bank of America (BAC), fell 1.3% to $50.56. That is a small decline, in line with a market that fell 1.0%, and it means the holding behaved roughly like the broad market this week. Ford (F) fell 6.6% to $11.60, a larger move than the market. Palantir (PLTR) was the other watchlist name that rose, gaining 5.2% to $196.75. Palantir and Tesla were the only two watchlist names that finished the week higher, out of ten that moved on the list.

Taken together, Eddie's two holdings had different weeks. GEV was a clear loser, and BAC tracked the market. The watchlist was weaker than the index overall, with eight of the ten names falling, and most of those falls were larger than the S&P 500's 1.0% decline. That tells him the watchlist is built more heavily from the segments that lagged, mainly technology and communication services. It means his names are more exposed to swings in those sectors than the index is.

The main event next week is the Federal Reserve's rate decision on Wednesday, October 28, at the end of its October 27-28 meeting. The Fed sets a short-term interest rate that influences borrowing costs across the economy. Stocks with high valuations or large growth expectations tend to be more sensitive to rate changes, because their expected profits are further in the future and lose more value when rates rise. Given that technology and communication services led the declines, the decision matters most for the parts of the market Eddie follows. Tesla's earnings and any Meta follow-up are the company events to watch alongside it.

There is also the SpaceX-Tesla situation to follow. If either company or its board confirms more detail, the stories from this week will turn from reports into facts, and the price moves will follow what is actually announced. If nothing further comes out, the excitement around the merger could fade. It is worth separating what Musk has said from what the boards have decided, since the coverage shows those are two different things. The lockup expiry is the other scheduled pressure point for SpaceX shares.

One idea this week illustrates is that a stock's price reflects expectations, not just results. SpaceX reported revenue up 161%, and the stock rose because the result was far above what analysts had predicted, by about $1.6 billion according to one headline. A beat means the company did better than the estimate, and the price moves on the gap between result and expectation. If expectations had already been very high, even strong numbers could have produced a selloff, because the news would have been already priced in. That is why a good report does not guarantee a gain, and why a weak report can still lift a stock that had been expected to do worse.
