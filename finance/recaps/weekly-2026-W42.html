Weekly market recap, week of Oct 12

The S&P 500 fell 2.0% this week to 7,659, but the damage was uneven. The Nasdaq lost 4.4% and closed at 26,140, while the Dow rose 0.5% to 52,002. When the Dow gains as the Nasdaq drops this much, the weakness is concentrated in one part of the market, not spread across it. The S&P sits between the two because it holds both kinds of companies. For you, the main point is that most of what you watch sits in the part of the market that fell hardest.

The sector numbers show where the pressure was. Technology fell 7.4%, far worse than any other group. Industrials lost 0.7% and communications lost 0.6%, which are small moves next to tech. Consumer staples gained 4.8%, energy gained 3.6% and materials gained 3.4%. Staples are companies selling things people buy in any economy, such as food and household goods, so money moving into them usually means investors are choosing lower-risk businesses over faster-growing ones. A week where staples lead and tech trails by this much is a defensive week.

One backdrop was in the news all week. Treasury yields were near a 24-year high while investors waited for inflation data and signals from the Federal Reserve. Yields matter to stocks because they are the return available on safe government debt. When they rise, a stock that promises most of its profit years from now becomes less attractive, because those distant profits are worth less in today's money. That is why growth and technology stocks are usually the most sensitive to rising yields. Tech's weekly loss fits that pattern, though no single headline proves yields were the cause. The same yields also bear on the banks you hold.

Dell was the largest loser among the names you watch, down 22.4% to 451.71. Barclays downgraded the stock after a sharp rally, meaning it lowered its recommendation after the price had already climbed a long way. One report had Dell down 8.0% on the day, and a later one called a drop of 11.8% its worst day in more than a year, with the Barclays downgrade as the likely driver. A downgrade after a big run is often about price: the analyst may like the business but think the stock already reflects the good news. It also puts attention on whether the profit margins on AI servers can hold up.

Bloom Energy, ticker BE, fell 19.6% to 221.31. One headline said the stock slipped overnight amid an AI selloff, and that on the same news cycle AEP was reported to be spending $4 billion on fuel cells to power Google. The stock was down 5.6% on that day. A large reported order for fuel cells would normally be good news for a company in that business, and the stock fell anyway. That tells you the sector selloff outweighed the deal on that day. It also means the order and the share price are telling two different stories right now.

TSMC gave the week's clearest example of good results meeting a falling stock. It reported third-quarter profit up 69% to a record, driven by demand for AI chips, and it was the sixth straight quarter of beating estimates. The stock still fell, by 3.6% to 5% on the day depending on the report, and finished the week down 9.0% at 412.61. The reports also tied the move to export-control and geopolitical worries, which are risks that a strong quarter does not remove. TSMC makes chips for much of the AI industry, so its numbers are a read on that demand. Strong demand did not protect the shares in a semiconductor selloff.

The rest of the chip group fell with it. Intel lost 12.4% to 89.85, AMD lost 11.9% to 528.75, and Nvidia lost 6.4% to 214.13. Nothing in the week's news gives a company-specific reason for Intel or AMD, so the best reading is that they moved with the group. Nvidia did have news: it was reported to be in advanced talks to acquire Reflection AI. That is a report of talks, not a signed deal, and it can still fall through. Separately, Microsoft unveiled its Maia 300 AI chip and plans up to one million units next year. Large customers building their own chips is a longer-term question for chipmakers, and it is not yet clear how it will play out.

SPCX fell 11.3% to 146.03, and two stories pulled in opposite directions. The FAA opened an environmental review of SpaceX's plan to launch 456 Starships a year, and the stock dropped roughly 4% to 5% that day. A review matters because regulators can slow or limit the expansion the company's growth depends on. Separately, SpaceX's purchase of spectrum, the radio frequencies that carry wireless signals, set off a selloff in telecom stocks, and T-Mobile pushed back on the deal. SPCX rose 1.4% on that news. The stock was among the most covered names this week with 33 stories, and the regulatory review looks like the larger of the two forces on the weekly number.

Tesla lost 7.3% to 354.72. A regulatory probe opened into Cybercabs speeding in school zones. This adds to the existing regulatory questions around the company's self-driving and robotaxi plans. A safety probe matters for a stock like this because the valuation leans heavily on those plans working at scale. The headline does not say what the probe will find or how long it will take, so the outcome is open. Part of the week's drop probably came from the broad tech selloff, but the facts do not let me split the move between the two.

The big banks reported, and the reactions were not what the results suggest. JPMorgan missed Wall Street's third-quarter estimates for both earnings and revenue, and the results included investment securities losses. Its shares rose anyway, by 0.4% in one midday report and 1.6% in another. Goldman Sachs reported profit up 29%, beat on trading revenue, and posted record advisory revenue, meaning fees for advising on deals. Its shares rose 3.3% in one report and 1.2% in another. Record advisory fees suggest the deal cycle is active, which matters because it is the business you are heading toward. Bonds already owned by a bank lose market value when yields rise, so the yield backdrop is a live issue for bank earnings.

Your two holdings both fell. GE Vernova lost 7.9% to 921.20, and Bank of America lost 6.0% to 51.23. The week's news includes no company-specific reason for the GE Vernova move, so it is most likely tied to the market-wide decline. Bank of America was the most-covered name of the week with 35 stories, yet its drop came in a week when JPMorgan and Goldman shares rose on their reports. Those gains were one-day reactions, while the 6.0% is a weekly figure, so the two are not directly comparable. Morgan Stanley, on your watchlist, lost 5.1% to 180.57. Banks as a group did not hold up well over the full week.

Meta was the clear winner in the news flow, though it is not in the weekly table, so I cannot give its weekly change. Its stock rose about 3.9% to 4.1% on a single day on retention data for its Muse product, and Zuckerberg described Muse Wonder as a frontier AI model. Truist then set a Street-high price target of $1,000 on Muse adoption, and the shares rose 3.2% on that. Snap rose with it, and one headline had Reddit down 3%. Retention data is the share of users who keep coming back, and investors treat it as evidence a new product is working. Meta gaining while most of tech sold off shows that the market was separating companies by their news and not selling everything equally.

Next week, the first thing to watch is the inflation data and the Fed signals that investors were waiting on. If yields stay near their 24-year high, the pressure on growth stocks may continue, and the banks will be affected too. No dated company events are confirmed for the week, so the open stories matter most. Those are whether Dell steadies after the Barclays downgrade, how far the FAA review of SpaceX goes, what comes of the Tesla Cybercab probe, and whether the Nvidia and Reflection AI talks become a signed deal. It is also worth seeing whether Bloom Energy and the other chip names recover once the sector selloff eases.

The idea this week teaches is that a stock price reflects expectations, not just results. TSMC reported record profit and beat estimates for the sixth straight time, and the stock still fell. Dell had rallied sharply before Barclays downgraded it, and it then had its worst day in more than a year. JPMorgan missed estimates and its stock rose. In each case the price had already moved before the news, so what mattered was whether the result was better or worse than what investors had assumed. A beat only helps the stock if it beats what was already priced in, and a miss can help if investors feared something worse. When you read earnings coverage, ask what the price already assumed before asking whether the numbers were good.
