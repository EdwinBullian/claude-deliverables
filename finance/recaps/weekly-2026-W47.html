Weekly market recap, week of Nov 16

The S&P 500 fell 8.0% this week and closed at 6,910, which is about 600 points below where it began. The Nasdaq lost 9.0% and ended at 22,618. The Dow lost 7.9% and ended at 48,147. A drop of this size in one week is rare for a broad index, and it matters because the S&P 500 is the benchmark most funds are judged against. Almost every stock Eddie holds or watches fell with it, and most fell by more.

The Dow is the index with the least technology in it, and it fell nearly as much as the other two. That means the selloff was not confined to one corner of the market. The Nasdaq's extra weakness shows that technology took the largest share of the damage, but the near-equal Dow decline shows that investors were selling across the market.

The sector numbers support that. Tech fell 10.6%, communication services fell 8.3%, and materials fell 7.4%. The best-performing sectors were consumer staples at 2.9% lower, utilities at 3.5% lower, and real estate at 4.7% lower. Staples and utilities sell things people buy in any economy, so their revenue is steadier, and they usually fall least when investors want less risk. None of the sectors in the best group actually rose. The difference between the best and worst sector was about eight percentage points, which is large for a single week.

Nvidia was the center of the week by a wide margin. It appeared in 159 news stories, far more than any other company, and AMD came next among the names Eddie follows with 42, then Tesla with 38. Nvidia is on Eddie's watchlist rather than in his holdings. It fell 21.1% to 125.53, the largest drop of any name he follows. Two separate stories drove that move, and they arrived together.

The first was a change in leadership. Headlines described Jensen Huang as having left the CEO role, with an interim CEO, Kress, preparing the company for its third-quarter earnings report. Other headlines said Huang was reportedly considering a return, that Elon Musk had joined calls to reinstate him, and that Nvidia's board opposed restoring him. These are reports, and the company's own position is only as complete as those headlines make it. A CEO change just before earnings matters because investors then have to guess at the company's direction and at who will make decisions about customers and spending. Uncertainty of that kind tends to lower the price investors will pay, even before any business results change. One headline also described a scandal around Huang, but that was a single unverified claim in a headline, so this recap does not treat it as established.

The second story was government action. Headlines reported that the US Commerce Department planned to curb access to advanced AI infrastructure and to block remote access to advanced AI chips, including access from China, with licensing rules covering five countries. Remote access matters because a customer can rent computing power in another country and use chips it could not legally import. Closing that route reduces the number of buyers who can use Nvidia's products. Trump acknowledged that his aides held different views on the new limits, so the final shape of the rules is still unsettled. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent also pressed Nvidia on who its next CEO would be and who gets its chips, and another headline said Trump would name Nvidia, Amazon and Microsoft as AI national champions. A government that decides who receives chips has real power over Nvidia's revenue, and that is a risk the stock did not carry a few weeks ago.

The export rules hit other chip companies in the same way. AMD and Broadcom shares dropped on the tighter chip rules, and CoreWeave and Nebius, which rent out computing power, dropped as well. AMD ended the week down 15.7% at 424.39. One headline put AMD's drop on a single day at 6%, which shows how much of the weekly decline came in concentrated bursts. AMD also completed its acquisition of World Labs and named Fei-Fei Li as chief scientist. Those are real company events, yet they did not protect the stock, because the sector-wide news about export limits and Nvidia carried far more weight that week.

Taiwan Semiconductor fell 16.0% to 318.71. A headline said it slid close to 6% on one day as Huang's exit and fears about AI debt rattled investors ahead of Nvidia's earnings. TSMC manufactures chips for the companies that design them, so it depends on those companies selling well. Another headline said Nvidia's financing role for neoclouds, the companies that rent out AI computing power, faced scrutiny after the CEO exit. That matters because it suggests Nvidia has been helping some of its own customers pay for its chips. If that arrangement is questioned, investors begin to ask how much of the demand is independent.

Dell fell 15.6% to 278.02. A headline described an 8% drop on one day in what it called an AI hardware selloff, with Hewlett Packard Enterprise down 4%. The same headline said a 2X leveraged Dell fund fell 16% that day. A 2X fund aims to deliver twice the daily move of the stock, so it falls twice as hard on a down day. That is why such funds are riskier than owning the stock itself, and why a 16% drop in one day is possible without any new information about the company. Dell sells the servers that hold the AI chips, so it moves with the chipmakers.

Palantir is the most useful example of the week. It raised its 2026 guidance and reported 96% revenue growth in the third quarter, driven by AI demand, and the stock still fell 13.4% to 143.49. A company raising its forecast is good news by any normal reading, and the facts here give no company-specific reason for the decline. The most consistent reading is that the stock was pulled down by the market and the AI sector, which also fell sharply in the same week.

Meta fell 12.4% to 560.84. Italy fined Facebook $887 million, and the stock dropped 2.4% on the day of that headline. That fine accounts for only a small part of the weekly loss, so most of the decline came from the same market-wide selling that hit the other names. Tesla fell 10.6% to 345.82, which is exactly the same as the tech sector's drop, and the facts given include no Tesla-specific story.

The two stocks Eddie actually holds both fell, but by different amounts. GE Vernova lost 10.8% and closed at 699.33, and Bank of America lost 7.5% and closed at 47.04. Bank of America fell slightly less than the S&P 500, while GE Vernova fell more. No story in the week's coverage explains either move, so the most honest reading is that both were caught in the broad decline. The bank names on the watchlist fell more than Bank of America did: Goldman Sachs lost 12.8% to 790.69 and Morgan Stanley lost 11.9% to 165.67. Those two earn a large share of their income from trading and deal activity, which tends to slow when markets are falling quickly. Bloom Energy also fell 19.4% to 142.39, and the facts for the week give no reason beyond the general selloff.

Next week, the first thing to watch is Nvidia's earnings and what it says about its leadership, since this recap has no information on how that report went. The second is whether the export rules are finalized, and in what form, given that Trump's aides disagree. The third is whether the AI selloff spreads further or stabilizes, which will show up in Dell, TSMC and AMD first. The calendar of scheduled events for the coming week was empty in the information available, so Eddie should check an earnings calendar himself.

The finance idea this week shows is that a stock's price reflects expectations, not just results. Palantir delivered 96% revenue growth and a higher forecast and still fell, which looks odd until you remember that investors had already paid a price that assumed strong results. Good news only raises a stock if it is better than what the price already assumed. A second idea sits beside it. In a sharp selloff, stocks tend to move together, because investors sell what is easy to sell and not only what has gotten worse. That is why a company with good news, like Palantir or AMD, can fall with the rest of its sector. Eddie can check this by comparing each name's company news with its weekly move: the news and the price changes did not line up.
