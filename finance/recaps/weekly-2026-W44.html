Weekly market recap, week of Oct 26

The S&P 500 closed the week at 7,580, down 0.8%. The Nasdaq fell 2.3% to 25,437, and the Dow lost 1.1% to 51,770. The gap between those numbers is the first thing to notice. All three indexes fell, but the Nasdaq fell nearly three times as much as the S&P 500. That means the damage was concentrated in the kinds of companies the Nasdaq is full of, not spread evenly across the market.

The sector data confirms it. Communication services fell 4.9% and technology fell 2.5%, the two weakest groups. Alphabet sits in communication services, and a Thursday headline said its drop was "dragging the Nasdaq with it." An index weights each company by its size, so when several of the largest companies fall together, the whole index moves with them. The S&P 500's smaller loss shows that a good part of the market held up while the large technology names sold off.

The best sectors were health care at +2.8%, materials at +2.6%, and utilities at +2.1%. Nothing in the week's news explains why those three led, so it would be a guess to assign a cause. What the numbers do show is that the market did not fall everywhere. Capital stayed in some industries while the most heavily covered technology stocks dropped, and that difference is worth tracking from week to week.

The biggest single story was Alphabet. It reported third-quarter results that disappointed, and the stock finished the week down 12.8% at $279.84. A headline said Alphabet was heading for a $400 billion drop in market value, the biggest in its history. Coverage pointed to two problems. Search results missed, and spending on AI pushed cash flow negative, with one account putting the cash burn at roughly $9 billion. GOOGL was also the most covered name of the week, with 62 stories.

The cash flow detail matters more than the search miss. Cash flow is the actual money a company takes in minus the money it spends. A company can report profits and still burn cash if it spends heavily on data centers and chips, because those costs are paid up front and only show up in earnings gradually. When cash flow turns negative, investors start asking how long the spending can go on and when it pays back. Alphabet is on Eddie's watch list, and that question is now the central one for the stock.

Meta raised the same question from another angle. It announced plans to raise up to $95 billion in equity to fund its AI build-out. That breaks down into a $35 billion offering of stock and preferred shares now, with up to $60 billion more through an at-the-market program, which one report says is planned for 2027. An at-the-market program lets a company sell new shares gradually into the open market instead of all at once. One headline noted that Meta stopped buying back its own stock in 2026 and is now selling it. META ended the week down 3.7% at $614.99, and the stock was down between 0.6% and 1.8% on the days the news was reported.

Selling new shares has a direct cost for existing owners, called dilution. When a company issues more shares, each existing share represents a smaller slice of the company, and the business has to earn more in total for the per-share value to stay the same. The raise also says something about the AI race. Meta is spending at a scale that its operations alone do not cover, and it chose to fund the gap by selling ownership instead of using only the cash it generates. One headline said analysts were offering a positive view, so the reaction was not uniformly negative, but the stock still fell.

Nvidia ended the week down 4.0% at $204.64. A report said CEO Jensen Huang was caught up in a Chinese spy probe, and the stock was down 1.1% on that news while AI stocks slid more broadly. The headline describes a report, and the facts given do not confirm what the probe involves. The reason it matters to shareholders is that a company's reputation and its dealings with governments can affect its sales and its ability to operate in certain markets. Nvidia is on the watch list, and the main thing to follow is whether the report develops into something with a clear legal or business consequence.

Intel had the largest weekly move on the list, down 25.2% to $65.76. The news attached to it was positive on the surface. New Street upgraded the stock, citing a multi-year shortage of server processors, and Intel rose about 5% to 6% on that day while AMD and Nvidia each gained about 3%. The stories provided do not explain how a stock with an upgrade ended the week down a quarter. The decline happened somewhere outside the headlines collected here, and Intel was the third most covered name with 44 stories. This is the item from the week that most needs a closer look, and the first step is to find the days and the cause of the drop.

GE Vernova, one of Eddie's two holdings, rose 3.6% to $857.87. It reported a profit that nearly tripled and a backlog of $196.7 billion, beat on third-quarter revenue, raised its 2026 guidance, and agreed to buy Trench Group for $9 billion. Backlog is the value of orders customers have committed to but the company has not yet delivered, so a larger backlog gives a clearer view of future revenue. Guidance is the company's own forecast for the year, and raising it signals management expects better results than before. The reports describe Trench as a grid business, which fits GE Vernova's power equipment focus. On the day of the report the stock was down about 2% anyway, a point the last paragraph returns to.

Bank of America, Eddie's other holding, fell 1.4% to $50.01. The financial sector as a whole rose 0.9% for the week, so BAC lagged its group instead of moving with it. The facts provided carry no news on the bank, so there is no company-specific explanation. A stock can trail its sector for reasons that never reach a headline, and a single week is a short window to read much into. For a holding, the useful habit is to note the gap and watch whether it persists over several weeks.

Several other names moved without much of a story attached. Tesla rose 4.2% to $399.81 after Barclays upgraded it, calling a SpaceX merger a key part of the bull case, and the stock jumped about 6.3% that day. Ford rose 5.4% to $12.25 and Bloom Energy rose 5.3% to $203.17, where the third-quarter call brought a guidance increase and a capacity plan. On the downside, Dell fell 9.0% to $424.30, SpaceX-linked ticker SPCX fell 8.3% to $134.72, and Palantir fell 7.0% to $184.11. The news collected for those three does not explain the drops. Microsoft, the second most covered name with 48 stories, offered voluntary buyouts to about 60,000 employees, roughly a quarter of its workforce, while it spends more on AI, and the stock rose 1.1% to 1.3% on the news.

Microsoft's move shows a different response to the same pressure Alphabet and Meta face. Buyouts reduce payroll costs, and the stock rose because the market liked a company trimming expenses to help pay for AI. Alphabet and Meta are spending on AI and absorbing the consequences in cash flow and share count. Microsoft is spending on AI and cutting costs elsewhere. All three are making large bets, and the market this week rewarded the one that showed how it would pay for it.

For next week, the most useful things to watch come from this week's stories. Alphabet needs to show whether the cash burn is a one-quarter event or a trend, and whether the stock stabilizes after a record loss in market value. Meta's first $35 billion offering has to be absorbed, and the planned $60 billion program is a reminder that more supply of shares could come later. Intel's drop needs an explanation, and the server processor shortage thesis will be tested against the price. Nvidia's probe report will either develop or fade. Dell, SpaceX and Palantir each fell sharply on the week, so any news that explains those moves is worth reading when it arrives.

The week also taught a concept that students often find confusing: a company can report good results and still see its stock fall. GE Vernova did exactly that. It tripled its profit, beat on revenue, and raised guidance, yet the stock was down about 2% on the day. The reason is that a stock price reflects what investors expected before the report, not just what the company delivered. If the market had already priced in a strong quarter, a strong quarter adds little new information, and any detail that falls short of the highest hopes can push the price down. The facts here do not say what expectations were, so the cause for this particular drop is not confirmed. The general lesson holds. A result is judged against expectations, and the same news can be good for the business and neutral or negative for the stock in the same session.
