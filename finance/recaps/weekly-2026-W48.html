Weekly market recap, week of Nov 23

The S&P 500 rose 2.1% this week to 7,297. The Nasdaq rose 2.3% to 24,130, and the Dow rose 3.5% to 50,775. The Dow led the three, even though the week's biggest story was about a technology company. A story like that usually lifts the Nasdaq most, so the Dow's lead means the gains reached beyond tech. The week was still shaped by one name. Nvidia drew 122 stories, nearly three times as many as the next most-covered company, Dell, with 45. Behind them came SPCX with 44, Tesla with 40, Alphabet with 27 and AMD with 23.

The Nvidia story began with a leadership change. The board reinstated Jensen Huang as chief executive after he had been out for 12 days. The stock jumped on the news, and the reported gain ranged from about 30% to about 34% depending on the source and the time of measurement. One report called it the biggest one-day gain in the stock's history. Another said Nvidia added nearly $1 trillion in market value, and a third said the rally added $2 trillion to the value of the Nasdaq 100. A chief executive matters to a stock because investors are pricing the person who sets strategy. Putting him back in the job removed a source of uncertainty, and the price moved sharply.

The coverage also said how the reinstatement came about. Trump gave the board assurances that Huang was not a national security threat, and Trump later said he had pressured the board. That makes this more than an ordinary corporate decision. A stock whose chief executive's status depends on a political assurance carries a risk that normal companies do not. Investors cannot easily estimate that risk, and they tend to demand a discount for risks they cannot estimate. It also explains why the move was so large. The change was abrupt and it came with a government angle, and the stock had been through what one headline called a brutal selloff before the jump.

The jump did not hold. The next day, reports said the rally was reversing and the wider AI trade sold off. Depending on when it was measured, Nvidia fell between 3.6% and 6.1%, and Intel fell with it by about 6% at one point. AMD dropped about 5% on the same day. Goldman said uncertainty lingered, and Bank of America called Huang's return a major catalyst while saying regulatory risks persist. The largest of those risks was reported to be an SEC accounting probe, which one headline said could be harder to shake than the chief executive question. A probe like that can lead to restated numbers, fines or a loss of investor trust, and a chief executive's return does nothing to end it.

For the full week, Nvidia closed at $150.49, up 4.9%. That figure shows how much of the one-day gain was given back. A stock that rose about 31% in a day and finished the week up under 5% went through a very large swing in both directions. The Nasdaq shows the same pattern. One report said the index rallied 6% on the Nvidia news, while its gain for the week was only 2.3%. The index and the stock both ended well below their peak for the week. For someone who watches Nvidia, the weekly number tells you where the stock stands, and the daily numbers tell you how unsettled it still is.

Dell was the clearest second-hand winner. It jumped about 20% in one day as AI hardware stocks rallied on the Nvidia news, and the report noted this happened one day before Dell's earnings. Dell closed the week at $316.96, up 4.4%, so most of that jump also faded by Friday. Dell sells servers built around Nvidia's chips, so news that lifts Nvidia tends to lift Dell as well. The timing matters too. A stock that has just risen 20% enters its earnings report with more optimism already in the price, and that makes the report harder to beat in the market's eyes. Next week's coverage should show how Dell's results were received.

Intel had a good week for its own reasons, and it was the one chip name with a story of its own. It landed its first customer for its 18A-P manufacturing process, which means another company has agreed to have Intel build its chips. Landing such a customer matters because Intel has been trying to prove that it can run a foundry business for other firms, and one signed customer is early evidence of that. The stock rose about 12% to 15% on the news. Intel closed the week at $57.40, up 5.7%, after also sliding in the Nvidia reversal. One customer is a start, and the stock will need more of them to keep the gain.

Tesla was the other big mover in the watchlist. It rose about 13% in a day as version 15 of its Full Self-Driving software rolled out, with Elon Musk calling it a "banger release." Tesla finished the week at $400.00, up 8.0%. Separately, Tesla and SpaceX named five directors for a potential merger that was described as worth $3.5 trillion. Naming directors is a concrete step in a merger process, and it shows the two companies are moving beyond talk. That story explains much of the coverage tied to SPCX, the other ticker in this week's tally. A merger of that size would take a long time to sort out, and shareholders of both companies would have to weigh what each side receives.

Amazon made a smaller but real deal announcement. It agreed to buy the technology and team of Decart AI in a reported $7.5 billion deal. Acquiring a team along with its technology is a common way for large companies to add AI talent faster than they could hire it. The headline described the price as reported, so the final terms may differ. The coverage did not say how Amazon's stock moved, so there is no weekly figure to give for it.

On the watchlist, the largest gain of the week belonged to Palantir. It rose 15.0% to $172.17, though no story in this week's coverage explains the move. Ford rose 9.4% to $12.33, and Meta rose 7.7% to $628.96. TSM, the chip maker, rose 4.5% to $353.34. None of these three had a story attached, so the moves cannot be tied to a cause here. It is worth noting that all of them rose in a week when the market gained, which means part of the move could simply be the market's general direction.

AMD was the clear loser among the names Eddie follows. It fell 10.0% to $424.54 for the week, in a week when the S&P 500 rose 2.1% and Nvidia, a direct competitor, finished higher. The coverage points to one day, when AMD dropped about 5% as the AI trade sold off. That accounts for roughly half of the weekly loss, and the other half has no stated cause. Goldman Sachs also lagged, falling 1.6% to $812.87 while the broader market rose. No story explained it, so it is a gap between that stock and the market and not a verdict on the bank.

Eddie's two holdings had quiet weeks. Bank of America rose 1.3% to $49.07, which is less than the S&P 500's gain of 2.1%. GE Vernova fell 0.3% to $735.31. Neither had a story attached, and neither had a news event that explains its move. The point is that both holdings sat out the week's biggest theme. Eddie's watchlist is heavy in the AI and chip names that swung the hardest, while his two actual positions are a bank and an energy equipment maker that did not move much.

The sector numbers add a puzzle. Materials led at +5.5%, followed by communications at +4.7% and consumer at +3.8%. Technology was the worst at +0.9%, with utilities at +1.1% and energy at +1.6% next. It is odd that tech was last in a week that centered on Nvidia, since the one-day jump in the biggest tech stock did not carry the sector. This fits the pattern in the stock data, where the Nvidia jump faded and chip names fell on the reversal day. Nothing in this week's coverage explains the strength in materials, so that gain stands without a stated cause.

For next week, the first question is whether the reversal in the AI trade continues or settles. Goldman's remark about lingering uncertainty and the SEC probe both point to Nvidia as the name where news could still move the whole group. The second is Dell, whose earnings reaction will show how the market treats a stock that rose sharply before its report. The third is whether Intel announces more foundry customers, since one customer is not yet a business. The fourth is the Tesla and SpaceX merger process, where the next step after naming directors will show how fast it is moving. The fifth is whether more details emerge on the Amazon and Decart deal.

The week's lesson is that a stock's price already contains what investors expect, so only news that differs from expectations moves it, and good news that was expected can still push a price down. Nvidia shows this on a large scale. Huang's return was a positive surprise because the market had not priced it in, so the stock jumped about 31%. Within a day, buyers who wanted the stock at the higher price had already bought, and the unresolved issues, such as the SEC probe, started to matter more. Dell enters its report after a 20% rise, so it needs to beat a higher bar, and a good result can still disappoint if the market hoped for more. When you read a headline, ask whether the news was already expected and what remains unknown, because the price reacts to that gap and not to the news alone.
