Weekly market recap, week of Nov 2

The S&P 500 fell 3.1% this week to 7,377, and the Nasdaq fell 4.0% to 24,579. The Dow Jones Industrial Average fell 1.4% to 51,286. The Nasdaq fell furthest because it is weighted toward technology companies, and technology was one of the weakest parts of the market. The Dow holds 30 large companies spread across more industries, and it fell the least. When the three indexes fall by different amounts, the gap shows which part of the market took the damage. This week it was tech.

Sector results point the same way. The best sectors were communication services at +4.4%, real estate at +1.9% and consumer at +0.6%. The worst were energy at -10.5%, technology at -8.7% and industrials at -3.8%. A sector figure is an average of the stocks in it, so a 10.5% drop in energy means most of its large companies fell hard together. No single headline this week explains the energy drop, so it is a fact to keep in view rather than a story with a known cause. Technology's drop is easier to read, because many of the individual names fell by double digits.

Eddie's watchlist took the heaviest losses. Dell fell 22.3% to 328.53, Bloom Energy fell 15.9% to 173.24, Palantir fell 14.0% to 160.38, Intel fell 13.9% to 56.28 and AMD fell 12.1% to 449.04. Tesla fell 11.5% to 358.22, Nvidia fell 10.1% to 186.64 and Taiwan Semiconductor fell 9.0% to 375.44. Losses that size in one week are large for companies of this size. They also happened across companies with different businesses, which suggests many investors were selling the technology sector as a group rather than reacting to one company's news.

Dell's drop was the biggest of the week, and there is no company news in this week's coverage that explains it. The same is true of Tesla, Nvidia, Taiwan Semiconductor and Intel, which also fell without a clear single cause. When a stock falls with its sector and no company news, the likeliest explanation is sector-wide selling, but that is an inference and not something the coverage confirms. Those five names are worth tracking next week to see whether they recover along with the sector or keep falling on their own.

The most important event for a stock Eddie owns was political. Democrats won Congress, and GE Vernova fell about 10% on the day while Vistra fell 12%. One headline quoted the reaction as "incrementally more negative sentiment." The concern is that a Democratic Congress revives policy worries about data centers and power supply. This matters because GE Vernova's recent business story depends on demand for electricity and grid equipment. A change in how investors expect Washington to treat that demand can move the stock even when nothing at the company has changed.

GE Vernova finished the week down 10.8% at 774.11, and Eddie holds it. The company's own news was good. It agreed to pay $9 billion for Trench Group, and the stock rose 2.3% on the day of that news. A separate report cited record orders and a data-center call from Bernstein, and the stock rose 6.5% that day. So the weekly loss came from the political selloff outweighing a good run of company news. The useful point for Eddie is that a company can report strong orders and still fall when investors change how they value the whole group of stocks tied to the same policy outlook.

Bank of America, the other stock Eddie holds, fell 2.0% to 49.42. That is a smaller loss than the S&P 500's 3.1%, and no company news explains it. Goldman Sachs, on the watchlist, fell 4.7% to 865.82, which is more than the market. The two banks moved by different amounts, so no single bank story covers both. Neither fell anywhere near as far as the technology names, which means the week's damage was concentrated in tech and not spread evenly across the market.

Apple was the most-covered name of the week, with 48 stories. It beat fiscal fourth-quarter estimates but missed on China revenue, and the stock fell roughly 2% to 4% across reports. The reason was the margin outlook. Apple warned that memory prices are "unprecedented," and higher memory costs will squeeze its profit margins in the holiday quarter. Margin is the share of each sales dollar left as profit, so a company can sell more and still earn less per sale. Investors paid more attention to that than to the earnings beat, because the stock price depends on future profits more than on last quarter's.

The memory warning also reached other companies. One headline said Apple's warning added fuel to a trade in Micron and SK hynix that was already wobbly. Apple also stockpiled $10 billion of parts while giving the warning, which shows it is spending heavily to manage the cost. For Eddie, the open question is whether higher component costs eventually reach the prices customers pay for devices. If they do, it could affect demand for Apple's products. If Apple absorbs the cost itself, the cost shows up in its margins, which is the outcome this week's selloff priced in.

Palantir is a second case of good results meeting a falling stock. It beat third-quarter estimates and raised guidance after its first $2 billion quarter. Revenue grew 96%, and commercial revenue led the growth. The stock still fell about 4% to 6% on the day and finished the week down 14.0% at 160.38. The concern was contract momentum. Reports pointed to slowing growth in new contracts and possibly shorter deals, which matters because contract growth is what sustains future revenue. Management said demand is "much larger than we can service," which suggests the limit is capacity and not interest from customers. The stock's reaction suggests investors want to see that limit ease.

AMD had better news than its weekly loss suggests. Its earnings eased fears about AI spending, the stock rose 4.7% on the day, and the Dow was close to a 2% gain at one point. AMD also doubled its long-term growth target for 2027. Even so, AMD finished the week down 12.1% at 449.04. A one-day gain after earnings does not offset a week of selling around it. Chip stocks also rose on Election Day, yet Nvidia, Intel and Taiwan Semiconductor all ended the week lower. A good day in a bad week left the sector, and the index, in the red.

Bloom Energy shows the same pattern from a different angle. It rose 11% on one day after a Barclays upgrade and a tie-up with California Resources aimed at one gigawatt of fuel-cell capacity. It also raised its full-year outlook after a 166% jump in revenue, yet the stock fell 5.5% on that day. Over the week it lost 15.9%. Amazon was the only watchlist name that rose, up 6.7% to 259.30. The coverage gives no cause for the Amazon gain, so it is a data point without an explanation.

Next week, the main things to watch come from what moved this week. First is whether the selling in technology continues or whether names like Dell, Nvidia and Intel stabilize. Second is whether power and data-center stocks, including GE Vernova and Bloom Energy, recover from the political worry or fall further as investors learn more about how a Democratic Congress will approach energy and data-center policy. Third is any further news on memory costs from Apple, Micron or SK hynix. Fourth is whether Palantir shows stronger contract growth. Fifth is whether AMD's improved outlook starts to show in its stock. No scheduled events are listed for the coming week, so these are open questions and not dated catalysts.

The week's main lesson is why a company can beat expectations and still see its stock fall. A stock price reflects what investors expect about the company's future profits, not only its last results. An earnings beat means the company did better than analysts predicted, but the market had already priced in some of that. What moves the price is the gap between results and expectations, and especially the outlook for what comes next. Apple beat estimates but warned about margins, so the future looked worse than investors had assumed. Palantir beat and raised guidance, but slowing contract growth suggested the future might not match the high expectations built into its price. When a stock has been valued as if everything will go right, a good result can still fall short of what investors wanted.
