The business

Broadcom sells two things. It designs semiconductors, and it sells infrastructure software, mostly VMware. In the fiscal third quarter ended August 2, 2026, semiconductors were $20.8 billion, or 70% of revenue, and software was $8.8 billion, or 30%. A year earlier the split was 57% and 43%, so the mix has moved quickly toward chips.

The semiconductor segment has two parts that behave very differently.

AI semiconductors were $16.7 billion in the quarter, up 221% from a year earlier and 54% from the prior quarter. That line was 56% of total revenue, up from 49% one quarter before. It has two products. XPUs are custom accelerators that Broadcom co-designs with a single customer for that customer's workloads, and they were 73% of AI revenue. The rest is mostly networking: Ethernet switch chips such as Tomahawk, plus optical components. Broadcom has only six XPU customers. On the call, management named Google, Anthropic, OpenAI and Meta and described each one's deployment plan.

Non-AI semiconductors, which include broadband, server storage and wireless, were $4.2 billion, up 5% from a year earlier and flat from the prior quarter. This is a mature business that now matters mainly as a source of stable cash.

Infrastructure software was $8.8 billion, up 29% from a year earlier, with annual recurring revenue growing 15%. Gross margin was 94% and operating margin was about 84%. Management guided next quarter's software revenue to about $8.7 billion, so the fast year-over-year growth is flattening on a sequential basis.

How Broadcom makes money comes down to operating leverage. Research and development and overhead barely move while revenue doubles. Non-GAAP operating expenses were about $2.1 billion in the quarter, close to the year-ago figure, against revenue that rose 86%. That is why operating margin reached 67.9% even as gross margin fell to 75%.

The gross margin decline is structural under this plan. The semiconductor segment's gross margin was about 67%. The CFO first said 76% and corrected it during Q&A, which is worth knowing because some coverage repeated the wrong figure. Management attributes the pressure to a growing share of XPUs that carry more memory content. Broadcom buys that memory and passes it through at a low margin. Fiscal fourth-quarter gross margin is guided to about 73%, down from 78% a year earlier, while operating margin is guided to about 66%.

Customer concentration is the first thing to understand about this company. Broadcom's top five end customers were about 55% of net revenue in the quarter, up from about 40% in the same quarter a year earlier. One distributor alone took 50% of direct sales in the quarter, though that figure measures a sales channel and does not identify the end buyer.

Industry and competition

What happened to the stock

The screen flagged Broadcom at about 1% of its 52-week range, with AMD, Micron and TSMC at 48-56%, and asked whether this was company damage or an overdone AI selloff. The answer is both, and the two parts cover different periods.

The sector event is recent and well documented. On Thursday, November 5, Broadcom fell 10.4% while the S&P 500 lost 4.4%, and the company released nothing that day. Coverage tied the move to post-election fear of a data center slowdown after Democrats' midterm victory, with new governors in major data center states promising construction moratoriums. Broadcom's fall sat between AMD's 17.3% and Nvidia's 9.3%, and it was smaller than the 12% fall in the SOXX semiconductor ETF. A partner at Pilot Wave Holdings said the day compressed AI capital spending, data center policy, inflation and interest rates into one trade.

The company-specific weakness is older. Broadcom's highest close was $481.57 on June 2, 2026, so $293.69 is about 39% below that peak (my math). The stock fell 12.6% on June 4 alone. It lost 31.7% over the three months to November 5, while the S&P 500 lost 5.0%. A 22.0% decline over one month implies a price near $376 a month ago (my math), which was already about 22% below the closing high. So the stock was well into a drawdown before the election. The low range position reflects real, multi-month underperformance, and I do not treat it as a quirk of the measure.

The November 6 rebound is the part I could not explain. Broadcom added less than 1% while AMD gained 11%, and the screen shows TSMC up 6.3% and Marvell up 5.7%. I found no source that gives the reason for that lag, and I am not going to assign one as fact. There are candidates. ARK reportedly sold about $134 million of Broadcom as the Nasdaq 100 fell, but that is very small against a market value near $1.4 trillion. A November 4 article described concern that Broadcom is losing Google TPU share to MediaTek. The backstops discussed below are the third candidate. My inference is that investors are treating Broadcom as the stock most tied to Anthropic's credit, which would be odd on a day when the Wall Street Journal reported that Anthropic passed $80 billion of annual recurring revenue, so this inference is weak (opinion/inference).

The competitive position

The market for AI compute has two approaches. Nvidia and AMD sell general-purpose GPUs. Broadcom, Marvell and MediaTek help large customers build custom chips. Customers choose custom silicon to lower the cost per unit of work. Management says XPUs run at half the cost of a GPU. That is the seller's claim and I could not verify it independently.

One trade source puts Broadcom above 70% of the custom accelerator market, and Fitch refers to its market share leadership in custom AI silicon and networking. I could not verify a current market share figure from a primary source.

Competition is getting more serious around Google, Broadcom's oldest XPU customer. TrendForce reported in August that Marvell struck a deal to help Google develop custom AI chips. Digitimes reports that MediaTek, Marvell and Broadcom are competing for ASIC supply through 2028. Hock Tan's response on the call was specific: Broadcom is shipping TPU version 8i ahead of MediaTek's version 8t, even though the MediaTek program started earlier. Management also says Google signed a long-term agreement covering future TPU generations and that it plans to deliver multi-tens of billions of dollars of TPUs annually for several years.

Networking is a separate source of strength. Broadcom was first to market with the 100-terabit Tomahawk 6 and has taped out a 200-terabit Tomahawk 7. Its networking is used in GPU clusters as well as XPU clusters, so some of that revenue does not depend on custom chips winning.

The binding constraint across the industry is physical deployment. Tan said land, power and shell are "more than a big concern" and dictate the timing of deployment. That links directly to the election issue. Nomura analysts wrote that much of the capacity due in 2027 is already under construction, while a larger share of the 2028 pipeline is still in planning and is more exposed to permitting delays. Goldman Sachs expects conditional moratoria in Texas, Georgia and Michigan but expects them to be short-lived if enacted.

The thesis

Four pillars, each with a check.

Pillar 1: Near-term AI delivery matches the guide. Broadcom has guided $21.7 billion of AI revenue and about 66% operating margin for the fiscal fourth quarter. Check: the December 9 report shows AI revenue at or above $21.7 billion and operating margin at or above about 66%.

Pillar 2: Anthropic's business supports the payments Broadcom guarantees. Anthropic's leases with Broadcom carry about $161.2 billion of rent over five years, excluding interest. Anthropic reportedly passed $80 billion of annual recurring revenue at the end of October. It reported its first quarterly operating profit in the second quarter of 2026 and a $567 million operating loss in the third. Check: the IPO completes, and quarterly revenue keeps growing without operating losses widening materially. I did not read the amended prospectus in full. A news summary of it puts total purchase and lease commitments at about $895.4 billion, so the Broadcom leases are a minority of what Anthropic has promised to pay. The reported first-half net loss of $80.7 billion is a headline figure, and Barron's has described it as not what it looks like, but I did not verify the components.

Pillar 3: Backstops stay inside the limits Fitch set. Fitch measures nominal backstop obligations plus reported debt less cash, and will revise the outlook to Stable above $150 billion and consider a downgrade above $185 billion. Today that figure is about $109 billion: $72 billion of backstops plus $61.1 billion of debt less $24.0 billion of cash (my math). Check: Broadcom's filings keep this figure under $150 billion.

Pillar 4: Custom silicon share holds at Google and networking keeps its lead. Check: TPU 8i keeps shipping in high volume on the December call, and no credible report shows Google moving a majority of a TPU generation away from Broadcom.

What the market may be missing

Everything in this section is my inference (opinion/inference).

The discussion of the backstop is focused on the smaller exposure. The first $35 billion deal has a maximum liability of about $29 billion at an 85% threshold, and the October $63 billion deal has about $43 billion at a 72.5% threshold. Fitch's estimate of the net liability after counting rack values peaks at roughly $14 billion to $15 billion. Fitch's release gives both figures in different sections. Against more than $30 billion of expected post-dividend free cash flow in fiscal 2026 alone, those numbers are manageable.

The larger exposure is on the income statement. If Anthropic deploys the 15 gigawatts described for 2027 and 2028 at $20 billion to $30 billion of content per gigawatt, that is $300 billion to $450 billion, which is about the same size as the entire $345 billion two-year guide. Those two figures cannot both be taken literally, since management said not all gigawatts ship in the window. Even so, the plain reading is that well over half the two-year AI plan could depend on one customer. A Broadcom investor is taking Anthropic revenue risk at a much larger scale than the guarantee implies.

The risks are also correlated. A lease default would most likely occur when AI demand is weak, which is when rack resale values would be low and when Broadcom's own revenue would be falling. Morningstar's low-concern view rests on this requiring both a default and a collapse in demand. That is accurate as far as it goes, but the two events tend to arrive together.

Adding every commitment overstates the guarantee. The $29 billion, the $43 billion, the $42 billion convertible facility and the $1.5 billion term loan total about $115.5 billion, more than Broadcom's $99.7 billion of equity (my math). But the convertible notes exist to fund lease payments, and lease payments reduce the backstop balance, so these amounts are not fully additive. Global Data Center Hub also cautions that the public record does not confirm that the October vehicle finances the same leases described in the S-1. Fitch assumes the convertible facility stays undrawn.

The guide is more exposed in fiscal 2028 than in fiscal 2027. Nomura's point about the 2028 pipeline applies directly to the $230 billion number, and most of the EPS gap between my base and bull cases comes from that year.

Risks and what would change my mind

Explicit breakpoints that would move me off the constructive view:

  1. AI revenue miss. Fiscal Q4 AI revenue below $21.0 billion, or a fiscal Q1 2027 AI guide below the Q4 result, would move me to neutral.
  2. Margin slippage beyond mix. A fiscal Q1 operating margin guide below 63% would mean operating leverage is no longer offsetting the gross margin decline, and I would cut the base-case EPS.
  3. More guarantees. TIKR reports Broadcom is weighing more than $50 billion of financing for OpenAI. A new backstop that pushes Fitch's measure above $150 billion, or any Fitch revision to Stable, would move me to neutral. This is the breakpoint I think is most likely to be tested. A backstop for OpenAI specifically would be worse than a further Anthropic one because management said it has "less clarity" on OpenAI's path.
  4. Anthropic's financing weakens. An IPO withdrawn or delayed past June 30, 2027, a sequential decline in Anthropic revenue, or any draw on the $42 billion convertible facility would count. A draw would mean the customer needed Broadcom's money to pay Broadcom's lease. I would move to negative on a draw.
  5. Customer loss. A credible report that Google has moved a majority of a TPU generation to MediaTek or Marvell, or that Anthropic has redirected a meaningful share of its 2028 plan to other suppliers, would break pillar 4.
  6. Data center restrictions that last. Moratoria in Texas or other major states that remain in force through mid-2027 would cut my base-case FY28 AI revenue.

Other risks I am carrying but not setting breakpoints for: supply limits on leading-edge wafers, substrates and memory; geopolitical disruption of the supply chain, which Fitch flags; and the chance that the AI correction Fitch assumes for fiscal 2028 arrives regardless of any one customer's health. Fitch also says the sustainability of the boom increasingly depends on interlinked customer, supplier and financing relationships, and a reversal in funding for the two leading labs could materially lower compute demand.

Finance idea of the week

The concept is a residual value guarantee, and Broadcom's backstops are a clean example.

A special purpose vehicle borrows money and buys racks of Broadcom chips. It leases the racks to Anthropic. The lenders look to Anthropic's rent first. If Anthropic defaults, the vehicle sells the racks. Under the October deal, Broadcom pays any difference between 72.5% of the amount still owed on the lease and what the racks sell for.

Take a lease with $100 still owed. If the racks sell for $40, Broadcom pays $72.50 minus $40, or $32.50. If the racks sell for $80, Broadcom pays nothing. Under the June deal, with an 85% threshold, the same $40 sale costs Broadcom $45. The lower threshold in the later deal means Broadcom took less risk per dollar of lease, and that is one reason the October deal could be larger.

In finance terms, Broadcom has written a put option on the racks, and the option is only live if the customer defaults. The seller of a put collects something up front and accepts a possible payment later. Here, what Broadcom collects is the chip sale itself, which might not happen at this size without the guarantee.

This matters for reading financial statements in three ways. The guarantee does not appear as debt on the balance sheet, so net debt to EBITDA does not capture it. The company reports the fair value of its first backstop as not material, which reflects a low assumed probability of default. And the maximum potential liability is a separate number from the expected loss. Broadcom's maximum is about $72 billion across the two deals. Fitch's estimate of the net exposure is far smaller because it subtracts the expected value of the racks, using a schedule in which they lose roughly two-thirds of their value by the end of year two. Fitch then runs a second, harsher test that assumes the racks are worth zero. When you see a contingent liability, look for all three numbers: the maximum, the analyst's expected loss, and what happens in the worst case.

The common mistake is to treat a guarantee as either a full debt or as nothing. Its value depends on how likely the default is and on how much the collateral is worth at the moment of default, and those two tend to get worse together.
