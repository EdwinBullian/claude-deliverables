Bottom line

Stance: positive on LRCX over a 12-month horizon (to late October 2027), medium conviction. Lam just reported record revenue of $8.22 billion and guided December higher to $8.6 billion. It also told investors it expects industry equipment spending to rise from the mid-$150 billions in 2026 to more than $200 billion in 2027. Even so, the stock fell 25% in a month and now trades near 24 to 25 times the earnings run-rate it guided for December, down from about 38 times forward earnings in February. I think most of the drop comes from sector-wide fear about AI funding, plus a December guide limited by supply and a China revenue decline the company had already flagged. The call is wrong if the January 2027 earnings call cuts the 2027 spending outlook below $200 billion, or if December gross margin falls below 51.5%, the bottom of the guided range.

The business

Lam Research makes the machines chip factories use to build chips. The fiscal 2026 10-K puts it this way: 'We are a global supplier of innovative wafer fabrication equipment and services to the semiconductor industry.' A chip is built by repeating two basic steps hundreds of times on a silicon wafer. First a thin layer of material goes down (deposition). Then the parts that are not wanted are cut away (etch). Lam is strongest in those two steps and also sells wafer-cleaning tools.

Its main tool families include ALTUS and Striker for atomic-layer deposition, VECTOR for dielectric films, SABRE for copper plating, and a range of etch systems. The latest call also discussed newer products. Akara does conductor etch. Aether is a dry photoresist used with EUV lithography. Syndion etches the vertical holes that connect stacked chips. Kallisto plates the large rectangular panels used in advanced packaging.

Who buys. The customer list is short and concentrated. The 10-K names Micron, Samsung, SK hynix and TSMC as the most significant customers in each of the last three fiscal years. In fiscal 2026 (the year ended June 28, 2026), four customers accounted for about 16%, 15%, 12% and 12% of revenue. That is about 55% of sales from four companies. Customers outside the US were about 93% of revenue in each of the last three fiscal years.

How it makes money. Lam reports two revenue lines. Systems revenue is the sale of new leading-edge tools. Customer support-related revenue comes from the Customer Support Business Group (CSBG): spares, service, upgrades of installed tools, and older-generation tools sold under the Reliant brand. In fiscal 2026, systems revenue was $14.89 billion and CSBG was $8.35 billion out of $23.23 billion total, about 64% systems and 36% support. In the September 2026 quarter, systems jumped to $5.73 billion while CSBG was $2.49 billion. That is a 70/30 split, because new-tool sales grew much faster.

The support line matters because an installed tool needs spare parts and service for many years. The more tools Lam has in the field, the bigger this recurring revenue gets. It depends on how hard customers run their existing factories (utilization), not on whether they are building new ones. CFO Doug Bettinger said on the call that spares are the biggest single piece of CSBG.

How a sale works. A chipmaker orders tools for a specific factory and process step. Lam builds, ships and installs them, and usually books the revenue around shipment. Japan is the exception. There, control passes only when the customer accepts the tool, so shipments sit in inventory until acceptance. The press release estimates about $468 million of future revenue from Japan shipments awaiting acceptance at the end of September. Deferred revenue, which is cash or billings received before revenue is booked, was $2.66 billion at quarter-end, up from $2.43 billion in June.

Why customers stay. The 10-K explains that once a chipmaker qualifies a supplier's tool for a step on a given technology node, the chipmaker 'generally maintains that selection' for that application. Switching is expensive because the chipmaker would have to re-qualify the process. So market share in a given step mostly changes when the industry moves to a new node or a new device design. That is why Lam talks so much about technology changes such as gate-all-around transistors, backside power, 3D DRAM and higher-layer NAND. Each one is a chance to win or lose tool positions.

End-market mix. In the September quarter, memory was 46% of systems revenue (NAND 26%, DRAM 20%), foundry was 46%, and logic and other was 8%. Memory systems revenue was up 120% year over year. Lam has historically been the most memory-heavy of the large US equipment makers, which is one reason the stock tends to move with Micron (opinion/inference, from the mix above and general industry knowledge).

Geography. China was 35% of September-quarter revenue, up from 26% in June, and 34% of fiscal 2026 revenue ($7.86 billion). Korea was 20% and Taiwan 18% in the quarter. Management said China will be a significantly lower share of revenue in December, mainly because domestic Chinese customers will buy less.

Industry and competition

Lam sells into the wafer fabrication equipment market, or WFE: the total chipmakers spend on front-end factory tools in a year. Lam estimates calendar 2026 WFE in 'the mid $150 billion range.' Its preliminary 2027 view is 'more than $200 billion,' with growth led by DRAM, then leading-edge foundry and logic, then NAND. That works out to roughly 30% growth in 2027, on top of a year that already grew strongly. Lam's 2026 estimate rose all year. Bettinger said the company started at the mid-$130 billions, moved to a bit over $140 billion, then a bit over $150 billion, and now the mid-$150 billions. His explanation was that customers squeezed more output from their existing cleanrooms and pulled projects forward.

Four demand drivers matter for Lam.

  1. AI accelerators need high-bandwidth memory (HBM). HBM uses more wafer area per bit than standard DRAM and needs extra etch and deposition steps for stacking. DRAM spending in the quarter focused on upgrades at the 1-alpha, 1-beta and 1-gamma nodes for DDR5, LPDDR5 and HBM.
  2. Leading-edge logic is moving to gate-all-around transistors and backside power delivery, and both add deposition and etch steps. Lam said it won tool-of-record positions for four products at a leading customer for backside power.
  3. Advanced packaging, the stacking and connecting of chips, is growing quickly. Lam expects its advanced-packaging revenue to grow about 70% in calendar 2026.
  4. NAND flash customers are upgrading tools to 200-plus-layer devices and starting to spend on 384-layer devices. Management also argued that a new low-latency SLC tier of NAND for AI workloads could raise the number of wafers needed. For each percentage point of NAND bits that shifts to SLC-class devices, about 2% more installed wafer capacity is needed to supply the same bits.

Lam says its served market, measured as a share of WFE, has expanded about four percentage points over the past few years. It expects 2026 to be its third straight year of growing faster than WFE, and it expects calendar 2026 systems revenue to grow 'well over 40%.'

Supply is also a constraint. CEO Tim Archer said cleanroom space and supply of certain components will keep limiting how fully chipmakers and equipment makers can meet demand in 2027. Bettinger said Lam expects 10 new Tier 1 fabs to come online during 2027. So the 2027 number depends partly on how fast new buildings are finished, not only on how much customers want to spend.

Competitors, from the 10-K:

  • Dielectric and metal deposition: Applied Materials is the primary competitor.
  • ALD and PECVD: Lam also competes with ASM International and Wonik IPS.
  • Etch: the primary competitors are Applied Materials, Hitachi and Tokyo Electron.
  • Wet clean: Screen, Semes and Tokyo Electron.

The 10-K also warns of two pressures from abroad. Government programs to build domestic equipment supply, including in China, could create more local competitors. And US export rules let foreign competitors sell to some Chinese customers Lam cannot serve. KLA is a natural valuation peer, but it sells inspection and measurement tools and rarely competes head-to-head with Lam (opinion/inference, general industry knowledge).

Peers are in the same cycle. According to 24/7 Wall St, Applied Materials' CEO expects another strong growth year in 2027, and Applied still gets 28% of its revenue from China. The equipment group sold off together this month. In the screen snapshot, KLAC fell 16.5% on the week, AMAT fell 12.3%, Micron fell 18% on the month, and the tech ETF XLK rose 0.5%. Equipment and memory fell hard while broad tech was flat. That pattern points to the market repricing the whole chip-spending cycle, not a Lam-specific problem.

Equipment demand is volatile because it depends on chipmakers' capital budgets, and those budgets move with memory prices and end demand. When chipmakers pause, new-tool orders can fall much faster than chip sales (opinion/inference, from the industry's history; none of this week's sources quantify past downturns). This is the main reason equipment stocks trade at lower multiples than their growth rates alone would justify.

Margins. GAAP gross margin hit a record 52.3% (52.4% non-GAAP), helped by customer and product mix, and operating margin passed 40% for the first time. Operating expenses grew much more slowly than revenue: $1.02 billion in the quarter versus $0.86 billion a year earlier, up 19% while revenue rose 54%. Taxes will lower EPS from here. The non-GAAP tax rate was 16.5% in September versus 11% in June. Management blamed a higher US tax on foreign earnings and shifts in where income is earned, and Bettinger said to expect that range for December 'and likely beyond.' With pre-tax profit held constant, moving from an 11% to a 16.5% tax rate cuts net income by about 6% (calculated as 0.835 divided by 0.89). The 10-K also shows a tax incentive worth about $0.77 per diluted share in fiscal 2026, so any change to that incentive is a real EPS sensitivity.

Cash flow quality. September free cash flow was a record $3.24 billion, more than the quarter's $2.75 billion of net income. Part of that came from working capital. Changes in operating assets and liabilities added $535 million in September after subtracting $860 million in June. Other current liabilities rose about $1.38 billion in the quarter, and Arcuri said payables were up about $1.3 billion. That fits Lam buying more parts to meet demand and paying for them later. The main reason was a $1.96 billion increase in receivables. In a fast-growing equipment company, cash flow trails earnings while receivables and inventory build, and catches up later. Over the trailing four quarters, free cash flow was about $6.5 billion on net income of about $8.4 billion (calculated).

The thesis

Pillar 1: industry spending in 2027 holds above $200 billion. Lam gave 'more than $200 billion' as a starting point and said it would give a sharper number on the January call. Archer said customer visibility extends through 2028. Check: the January 2027 call keeps the 2027 WFE outlook at or above $200 billion.

Pillar 2: Lam keeps growing faster than the industry. The drivers can be measured:

  • advanced packaging, guided up about 70% in 2026
  • NAND upgrades, which management says continue into 2027
  • foundry/logic and DRAM products, about 80% of this year's systems growth
  • Aether dry resist, whose cumulative revenue target rose by $1 billion to about $2.5 billion by 2030

The Aether figure is small next to about $29 billion of annual revenue. Its importance is in winning positions at new nodes, not in the near-term numbers (opinion/inference). Check: the March-quarter guide given in January should show growth from December, since shipments delayed by supply constraints move into March. The longer-term check is whether calendar 2027 systems growth beats 2027 WFE growth.

Pillar 3: margins hold as China revenue falls. Management guided China lower for December and named customer mix as a gross-margin headwind for the same quarter, which suggests Chinese customers have been helping margins (opinion/inference). If gross margin stays near 52.5% while China's share falls, the margin gains came from products and cost, not only from mix. Check: December-quarter gross margin within the 51.5% to 53.5% guide range, and operating margin at or above 39.5%.

Pillar 4: the price already reflects a slowdown. At about 24 to 25 times run-rate earnings, with the multiple down about a third since February, the stock prices in a real chance that 2027 disappoints (opinion/inference). Capital return also supports per-share earnings: the 85%-of-free-cash-flow target, the active accelerated buyback and the 27% dividend increase. Check: if Lam meets its December guide and the stock is still below $230 when it reports in January, the multiple has compressed further while the numbers held or rose.

What the market may be missing

Everything in this section is opinion/inference built on the sourced facts above.

Supply set the December guide. Bettinger said the guide would have been 'a bit higher' without supply constraints, and that shipments missing December because of timing will ship in the March quarter. Arcuri asked whether the gap was $300 to $400 million, and Bettinger declined to give a number. If the gap is real, that revenue moved from one quarter to the next and was not lost. The market read the constraint as a cap on the cycle. I read it as a timing shift, unless the March guide fails to show the catch-up.

The China decline hides strong growth elsewhere. Management guided China to a significantly lower share in December, yet total revenue is guided up about 5%. Non-China revenue in September was about $5.34 billion (65% of $8.22 billion). If China falls to 25% to 28% of December revenue, non-China revenue would be about $6.2 to $6.45 billion, up roughly 16% to 21% in one quarter. The 25% to 28% range is my assumption; the company did not give a number.

The support business grew 40% and is steadier than the rest. CSBG is about 30% of revenue, set records four quarters in a row, and is guided to grow more than 30% in 2026 and more than 15% in 2027. Spares and service depend on factory utilization rather than new construction, so this revenue should fall much less than systems revenue in a downturn. At roughly $10 billion a year (my estimate from recent quarters), it is a large and comparatively stable part of a volatile business.

The DRAM design scare looks overdone. On October 21 the stock traded down after a memory maker's investor call. Arcuri said on Lam's call that the company signaled it would prioritize 3D DRAM over 4F² designs. A search-result summary attributes to Micron's CTO a comment that 4F² offers little density benefit over 6F². Archer answered that a direct move to 3D DRAM would raise deposition and etch intensity even faster and sooner, which helps Lam. I could not verify the memory maker's full remarks. Lam's argument holds up on its face, because three-dimensional structures need more deposition and etch steps.

Where the market may be right. This sell-off is part of a wider pullback in the AI trade. Yahoo Finance headlines this week said last week saw the largest tech sell-off in more than five years, and that CoreWeave and Nebius fell more than 25% in a week on AI financing fears. Lam's own rule of thumb helps size the risk: about $100 billion of AI capex creates about $10 billion of WFE. A $100 billion cut to planned AI spending would therefore remove about 5% of a $200 billion WFE year. That matters but would not break the thesis alone. The bigger risk comes second. If AI demand slows and memory prices fall, memory makers cut capex, and memory is nearly half of Lam's systems revenue.

Risks and what would change my mind

Each breakpoint below can be observed. If one is hit, I re-underwrite and expect to move to neutral or negative.

  1. 2027 WFE outlook cut. If the January 2027 call puts 2027 WFE below $200 billion, pillar 1 breaks. A number in the $180 billions would mean 2027 growth near 17% instead of 30%.
  2. Margin miss. December gross margin below 51.5% or operating margin below 39.5% would mean the margin gains depended on China mix.
  3. Delayed revenue not recovered. If the March 2027 quarter guide is at or below December's actual revenue, my timing-shift reading of the supply constraint is wrong.
  4. AI capex pullback. If two or more of the largest US cloud spenders lower their 2027 capital spending plans, or neocloud funding problems lead to project cancellations, the 2027 WFE number is at risk.
  5. Memory capex cuts. Micron, SK hynix and Samsung are among Lam's largest customers. A 2027 capex cut from any of them hits the memory half of systems revenue directly.
  6. China policy. The 10-K describes US export controls and entity listings that already restrict sales to some Chinese customers, and says more could be added. China was 34% of fiscal 2026 revenue. New restrictions that remove a large share of domestic Chinese revenue, with nothing to offset it, would cut both revenue and margin.
  7. Supply chain and rare earths. The 10-K says China's export controls on certain rare earth elements are partly suspended until November 2026 unless extended. If the suspension lapses, component supply could get tighter.
  8. Customer concentration. Four customers are about 55% of revenue, so one large customer delaying a fab would visibly move Lam's numbers.
  9. Multiple compression without an earnings miss. Even if Lam delivers, the market can decide to pay less for peak-cycle earnings. At Applied's roughly 20x forward multiple, Lam would be worth about $186 on the $9.32 run-rate (calculated), about 19% below today.

Three things would make me more positive: a December beat with a March guide above $9 billion, a 2027 WFE number raised above the 'more than $200 billion' starting point, or evidence that supply constraints are easing.

Finance idea of the week: operating leverage

Operating leverage is how much a company's operating profit changes when its revenue changes. It comes from costs that do not rise one-for-one with sales. Lam's last year shows it clearly. From the September 2025 quarter to the September 2026 quarter:

  • Revenue rose from $5.32 billion to $8.22 billion, up 54%.
  • Gross profit rose from $2.68 billion to $4.30 billion, up 60%, because gross margin improved from 50.4% to 52.3%.
  • Operating expenses (R&D plus selling and administrative) rose from $0.86 billion to $1.02 billion, up only 19%.
  • Operating income rose from $1.83 billion to $3.28 billion, up 80%.

Divide the change in operating income (80%) by the change in revenue (54%) and you get about 1.5. That ratio is the degree of operating leverage: each 1% of revenue growth produced about 1.5% of operating income growth. The incremental margin shows the same thing. Lam added $2.90 billion of revenue and $1.45 billion of operating income, so about 50 cents of each extra revenue dollar became operating profit. A year earlier, its operating margin was 34%.

For an investor, the key point is that operating leverage also works in reverse. Here is an illustration only. Suppose Lam's revenue fell 20% from the September level to $6.58 billion, and gross margin slipped to 50% as its factories ran below capacity. Operating expenses stay at $1.02 billion, because R&D programs are hard to cut quickly. Gross profit would then be $3.29 billion and operating income $2.27 billion. Operating income would fall 31% on a 20% revenue decline.

This is why cyclical companies often look cheapest on P/E right at the peak. At the top of a cycle, operating leverage inflates the 'E' in P/E, so a low multiple at that point can be a warning sign. Experienced equipment investors therefore ask what the company would earn in a normal or weak year, and what multiple that deserves. My bear case above, $7.40 of EPS at 20 times, is that question applied to Lam. Today's 24 to 25 times run-rate multiple is not cheap for a peak year. The bull argument is that 2026 is not the peak, because 2027 spending is guided about 30% higher. The January call will test that argument.
