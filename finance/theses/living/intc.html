_Finance thesis desk · Sunday stock report · 2026-11-01 ET · Re-underwrite of the living thesis dated 2026-10-12 · Prices are the Friday 2026-10-30 close (4:00 PM ET) · Recommendation only. Eddie places all trades manually._

Bottom line

The old neutral stance holds. It does not flip and it does not close, because the cause of Friday's 28.6% drop is now known: Intel cut its outlook for next quarter because of a PC inventory correction, limits on server supply and a fatal accident at its Israel fab. None of that breaks the foundry-customer or CHIPS-funding thread the 2026-10-12 memo was watching. The horizon is six months, through the Q1 2027 report expected around late April 2027, with the Q4 2026 report in late January 2027 as the first checkpoint (neither date is announced yet). Conviction on neutral rises from low to medium. The data center business grew 78% and cannot get enough supply, but at $65.76 the stock still trades at about 31 times Q3's annualized adjusted earnings, free cash flow is guided negative through 2027, and there are about 20% more shares than a year ago. Before turning constructive I need a named external foundry customer or evidence that the PC decline has bottomed.

What happened on Thursday and Friday

Intel reported Q3 2026 after the close on Thursday 2026-10-29. The quarter beat. Revenue was $16.5 billion, up 21% from a year earlier, and adjusted (non-GAAP) EPS was $0.52 against the $0.40 expected by analysts polled by LSEG. The outlook missed. Intel guided Q4 revenue to $15.0 to $16.0 billion and adjusted EPS to $0.25, against analyst expectations of $17.1 billion and $0.43. The stock closed at $92.07 on Thursday and $65.76 on Friday, down 28.58%. Yahoo Finance looked at closing prices back to May 1973 and found only one worse day in Intel's history, July 5, 1974. About $140 billion of market value was lost in one session. INTC is down 23.1% for the week and 45.3% for the month, and sits at 29.9% of its 52-week range of $32.89 to $142.35. Before the report it was still up about 150% for the year.

Scoring the 2026-10-12 memo

That memo saw a 12.75% weekly drop that hit only Intel, had no explanation for it in its inputs, and declined to re-rate. Three parts of it can now be checked.

  1. It said a break below about $106 would mean the selloff had gone further than unwinding the September rally. That happened. Friday's close is 37% below the $104.70 price in that memo (arithmetic).
  2. Its unscored lean was that the drop reflected real company-specific news. That was only partly right. CNBC attributes Intel's roughly 23% October decline before earnings to concern about the size of the AI buildout, which is a sector-wide worry. The company-specific news came with the Q3 report.
  3. It listed four possible causes: losing a foundry customer, a CHIPS change, a capital action, or the rally unwinding. The real cause was one it did not list: a guidance cut on the earnings date. Earnings dates belong on the breakpoint list of every living thesis, because the quarterly guide is the most common trigger for a one-day move this large in a big company (opinion/inference).

On the open thread itself: no foundry customer was lost, because Intel still has no major external foundry customer to lose (CNBC). Nothing in the release, the prepared remarks or the 10-Q pages I read shows a change to CHIPS funding. Intel completed the RAMP-C program this quarter, and escrowed shares are still being released to the Department of Commerce as Intel performs under its Secure Enclave agreement. The thread stays open, and it now has a specific test. On the call, Tan said Intel will have “a couple of customers ready to announce the next two quarters.”

The business

Intel designs processors, the chips that do the general-purpose computing inside PCs and servers. It makes most of them in its own factories, called fabs. Owning fabs is the main difference between Intel and AMD or Nvidia, which design chips and pay TSMC to make them. It also explains most of what happened to the stock this week, which the last section covers. Intel reports three groups.

Intel Products is the chip business. It has two segments.

  • Client Computing and Physical AI Group (CCPG) was called the Client Computing Group until this year. It sells processors for laptops and desktops plus chips for edge devices. Q3 revenue was $8.1 billion, down 5% from a year earlier, down 9% from Q2, and below Intel's own expectations. Operating profit was $2.3 billion, a 28% margin. The customers are PC makers; Intel named Acer, ASUS and Lenovo in its Googlebook announcement this quarter. PC makers buy processors before they build laptops, so they hold inventories of Intel chips. When they expect weaker sales, they buy fewer chips than they ship out as finished PCs, and Intel's revenue falls faster than PC sales to end users. Intel says this is happening now: its OEM partners are “aggressively managing inventory to match the softer sell thru.”
  • Data Center and AI (DCAI) sells Xeon server processors, custom chips built for single large customers (Intel calls these purpose-built silicon), and a new inference GPU called the D-series. Q3 revenue was $7.3 billion, up 78% from a year earlier and up 17% from Q2, the segment's fastest growth in 15 years. Operating profit was $3.0 billion, a 42% margin. Growth came from hyperscale cloud companies, enterprises and the newer GPU cloud providers (neo-clouds). Purpose-built silicon revenue more than doubled year over year. Intel says DCAI demand “continues to meaningfully exceed available supply.” It also says DCAI revenue is on track to finish 2026 nearly $10 billion above what it expected at the start of the year.

Intel's argument for why AI helps a CPU company goes like this. AI agents carry out multi-step tasks instead of answering a single prompt, and they need CPUs to coordinate those tasks and move data. Management estimates the server CPU market could grow from $40 billion to more than $200 billion over five years. That figure is management's estimate, not a measured fact. It is the most important number in the bull case and also the hardest to verify.

Intel Foundry is the manufacturing arm. Q3 revenue was $6.7 billion, up 58%, but almost all of it was wafers sold to Intel Products. External foundry revenue, from chips made for other companies, was $411 million, and Intel says part of that came from a one-time program. Foundry's operating loss was $1.9 billion, $236 million smaller than in Q2. Its process nodes, oldest to newest, are:

  • Intel 3, used for current server chips.
  • Intel 18A, in high-volume production in Arizona.
  • Intel 18A-P, launching across Intel's own products by year-end.
  • Intel 14A, whose design kit went to select customers in October, with risk production planned for 2027.

Advanced packaging joins several chips into one package (Intel's version is called EMIB). Today it is the part of foundry where Intel reports external customer traction.

All Other ($0.7 billion) holds Mobileye and IMS. It shrank 30% because Intel sold 51% of Altera in September 2025 and stopped consolidating it.

Why the segments add up to more than the total. CCPG ($8.1B), DCAI ($7.3B), Foundry ($6.7B) and All Other ($0.7B) add up to $22.8 billion, but total revenue was $16.5 billion. The $6.3 billion gap is intersegment eliminations. Foundry's sales to Intel Products happen inside the company, so they cancel out in the consolidated total. When you read that “Foundry grew 58%,” it mostly means Intel Products bought more wafers from its own factories.

The mix is shifting. In Q3, CCPG was 53% of Intel Products revenue and DCAI was 47%, but DCAI already earned more operating profit ($3.0B against $2.3B). For Q4, Intel guides DCAI up by a mid-teens percentage from Q3, about $8.4 billion, and CCPG down more than 20%, below $6.5 billion (arithmetic on guidance). If both hold, the server business will be bigger than the PC business in Q4, a point the Motley Fool also makes.

Ownership. The U.S. government took a 10% stake in Intel last year, and Nvidia and SoftBank each invested billions. In August Intel sold $23 billion of new stock in a public offering, and Tan says it brought in sovereign funds and long-term investors. Some Intel shares are held in escrow. They go to the Department of Commerce as Intel performs and receives cash under its CHIPS Act Secure Enclave agreement. That escrow is where the CHIPS thread shows up in the income statement, as the numbers section explains.

Industry and competition

Intel competes in three markets.

PC processors. The PC market is shrinking because memory is expensive. Memory costs rose sharply this year, and Intel says that has pushed up the price of finished PCs “significantly,” which squeezes IT budgets that were already tight. Counterpoint Research, cited by CNBC, counted a 4% year-over-year drop in global PC shipments in Q2. Intel now expects 2026 PC units to fall about 12%, with second-half units down more than 20%. It is planning for another drop of about 10% in 2027, steeper in the first half, and does not expect a lasting recovery until memory supply improves and prices normalize. Cantor Fitzgerald's C.J. Muse wrote that current DRAM prices imply a 45% year-on-year fall in PC units, which he called a meaningful surprise.

Intel's products look stronger than the market they sell into. Wildcat Lake, a lower-cost 18A laptop chip, is sold out. AI PC revenue grew 35%. Nova Lake is running ahead of schedule, and Intel promised more details at CES in January. My sources do not cover PC processor market share, so I am not estimating share gains or losses against AMD or Arm-based chips.

Server processors. This market is growing fast and is held back by supply. Intel expects industry server CPU units to grow more than 30% in both 2026 and 2027, and expects the industry to stay short of supply through 2027 and into 2028. Intel's own constraints are:

  • yields and cycle times on Intel 3,
  • an industry shortage of the advanced substrates its packaging uses,
  • and now the Fab 28 shutdown.

The main competitor is AMD. Cantor's Muse listed lost server share to AMD as one of the quarter's problems. Tan acknowledged that “nearly every fabless company races to meet this unprecedented demand with their own offering.” That would include the big cloud companies building their own Arm-based chips (opinion/inference: the remark does not name them). AMD reports next week, which will give a direct read on server share.

Foundry. TSMC dominates advanced chip manufacturing. Intel describes itself as one of three advanced-node foundries in the world and the only one based in the United States. It is in high-volume 18A production in Arizona, and its next Arizona fab is expected to start operating by the end of 2028. A fab is a large fixed cost. It earns a return only when it runs close to full, and Intel's own products are unlikely to fill a leading-edge fab alone (opinion/inference). That is why an external customer matters so much to the long-term case, and why the lack of one has been the open thread for months. Tan says he will not disclose customer details, which he calls standard foundry practice. On the same call he said customers would be ready to announce within two quarters. In September, reports said SK Hynix might lease foundry space at Intel's Ohio site to make memory. Neither company confirmed it, and Tan said only that he hopes to share more on Ohio “in the near future.”

Suppliers. Intel announced work with ASML on getting ready for High-NA EUV, and new facilities with Lam Research and Applied Materials. It said firm purchase orders to equipment suppliers rose nearly 40% from Q2. Intel's capex is revenue for those suppliers, which matters for LRCX, AMAT and ASML on the watchlist (opinion/inference).

Why GAAP and non-GAAP disagree. For the nine months, Intel reported a GAAP net loss of $11.6 billion, while its adjusted earnings were positive. The biggest single reason is a $12.6 billion non-cash loss from marking the escrowed shares to market. Intel carries those shares as a liability valued at the current stock price. When INTC rose about 150% this year, the liability grew and Intel booked a loss. Intel leaves this out of its non-GAAP results. This has two effects:

  • Q3 GAAP EPS ($0.60) came in above non-GAAP EPS ($0.52) partly because of a $120 million gain on that liability.
  • Friday's drop reduces the value of the liability, so Q4 GAAP results may include a gain that has nothing to do with operations (inference). Keep that in mind when reading Q4 GAAP EPS.

Dilution. Intel guides 5.44 billion diluted shares for Q4, about 20% more than in Q3 2025. With that many more shares, net income can grow 20% and EPS stays flat. This is why growth per share will lag growth in total dollars.

The thesis

The re-underwritten thesis is neutral. Intel's progress in servers and manufacturing is real and mostly reflected in the price. The PC reset, negative free cash flow and the missing foundry customer limit the near-term upside. There are four pillars, each with a test.

Pillar 1. DCAI demand exceeds supply, and Intel is adding supply. Evidence: 78% growth, demand above supply, server units forecast up more than 30% in both 2026 and 2027, and an industry short of supply into 2028. Test: Q4 DCAI revenue grows at least 10% from Q3 despite the Fab 28 hit. Intel guides mid-teens growth, about $8.4 billion. Growth below 10% would mean Intel is failing to execute on supply, which is Seaport's concern.

Pillar 2. Manufacturing costs keep falling. Evidence: 18A costs down 40% since Q1, Intel 3 costs down 15%, and yields ahead of plan. Test: Q4 non-GAAP gross margin comes in at or above the 38% guide, and the Q1 2027 guide is not below 37% after the second Fab 28 quarter. A lower guide would mean the cost of idle fab capacity is outweighing the cost gains.

Pillar 3. The PC decline is an inventory correction that bottoms in the first half of 2027. Evidence: Intel says PC makers are cutting the number of weeks of inventory they hold, so Q4 shipments to them are below end demand. Intel plans for the 2027 PC unit market to fall about 10%, with possible improvement in the second half against easier comparisons. Test: Q4 CCPG revenue falls no more than about 25% from Q3, and the Q1 2027 guide does not cut the 2027 PC market forecast below minus 10%. A further cut would mean the problem is demand, not inventory.

Pillar 4. Intel Foundry signs an external customer. Evidence: the 14A 0.9 design kit is released, 18A-P is on track, and Tan said customers will be ready to announce within two quarters. Test: a named external customer for 18A, 18A-P or 14A wafer manufacturing by 2027-04-30. I lean slightly toward management delivering, because Tan made a dated statement on a public call, but my confidence is low. Without a customer, the foundry segment stays an internal cost center losing about $1.9 billion a quarter.

Why neutral and not bullish. Pillars 1 and 2 are working, and they are why the stock rose about 150% this year. Pillars 3 and 4 are unresolved, and the price already gives credit for a recovery. Intel guides free cash flow negative through 2027, so a long-term holder depends on the earnings recovery arriving on schedule.

Why not bearish. Intel has beaten its own guidance eight quarters in a row, which makes it more likely than not to meet the Q4 guide (opinion/inference). It has about $54 billion of cash and short-term investments and slightly more cash than debt. The U.S. government owns about 10%, which lowers the risk of a funding crisis (opinion/inference). And every analyst target in the post-report set, including the one Sell, is above the current price.

Predictions. I expect Q4 revenue at or above the $15.5 billion midpoint (medium confidence). I expect the stock to close below its $92.07 pre-earnings price on 2027-01-29, the last trading day before the likely Q4 report window (medium confidence). I expect Intel to name at least one external 18A, 18A-P or 14A wafer customer by 2027-04-30 (low confidence).

What the market may be missing

Everything in this section is opinion/inference built on the sourced numbers above.

  1. Q4 understates PC end demand. Intel guides CCPG down more than 20% from Q3 while it expects second-half PC units down more than 20% year over year. Part of the Q4 drop is PC makers cutting inventory, and those cuts stop once inventory reaches the level they want. After that, Intel's PC revenue should follow end sales again, and those are forecast down 10% in 2027, not 20%. If the market is annualizing Q4, it is too pessimistic about 2027 PC revenue.
  1. Fab 28 costs two quarters, and the second one is still ahead. The Q4 guide includes about $300 million. CNBC's figure of $600 million over two quarters implies roughly another $300 million in Q1 2027. The Q1 guide in January may therefore look weak for a reason that has already been disclosed, and the market may read it as new deterioration.
  1. Inventory risk. Seaport argues Intel made too many client wafers and too few server CPUs. Inventories rose $1.45 billion this year to about 127 days of cost of sales, and Intel is now cutting client wafer starts. If some of that inventory is older client product in a falling PC market, an inventory write-down in Q4 or Q1 is possible. Intel said lower inventory charges helped CCPG profit in Q3, so those charges can also rise and hurt.
  1. Q4 GAAP results may look better than operations. The fall in INTC shrinks the escrowed-share liability, which may produce a GAAP gain. Intel guided GAAP EPS of $0.10 against non-GAAP EPS of $0.25, which suggests the guide, set before Friday's drop, includes no such gain. If Q4 GAAP EPS beats by a wide margin because of that gain, ignore the beat.
  1. Per-share results lag dollar results. There are about 20% more shares than a year ago, and 2027 capex will be “up strongly.” Even if net income doubles from Q4's level, the extra shares hold back EPS growth, and another equity raise would do the same again.
  1. Capex tells you how management sees server demand. Intel is raising capex while earnings fall, and it says it will not spend a dollar of capital without clear evidence of customer demand. Either management can see demand the market is not crediting, or it is building too much capacity ahead of a downturn. On the Q4 call, firm purchase orders and the 2027 capex number should show which.

Risks and what would change my mind

Breakpoints that would flip the stance to bearish:

  • Q4 revenue below $15.0 billion (the bottom of guidance), or non-GAAP gross margin below 37%.
  • Q4 DCAI revenue up less than 10% from Q3, which would mean supply limits are getting worse while competitors take share.
  • Intel cuts its 2027 PC market forecast below minus 10%, or pushes the expected end of the PC slowdown past the first half of 2027.
  • An inventory write-down above $500 million in Q4 or Q1 2027 (my threshold for a material charge).
  • Another stock offering or a convertible bond within 12 months of the August raise.
  • Any change to CHIPS Act funding or to the Secure Enclave terms, including a pause in escrowed-share releases.
  • AMD reports next week a server CPU share gain big enough that its server revenue grows faster than Intel's DCAI.

Breakpoints that would move the stance to constructive:

  • A named external customer committing volume to 18A-P or 14A.
  • Q4 revenue at or above $16.0 billion with non-GAAP gross margin at or above 39%, plus a Q1 2027 guide showing CCPG stabilizing.
  • An Ohio agreement in which a partner such as SK Hynix puts in its own capital, reducing Intel's capex burden.
  • A price in the low $50s with pillars 1 and 2 still intact. At that price the multiple on 2027 consensus would be about 24 times, and the balance of risk and reward would justify revisiting the stance (opinion/inference).

Risks the breakpoints do not cover: a broader pullback in AI spending (the October selloff was partly about this), Middle East conflict affecting Intel's Israeli fabs, U.S.-China trade policy, and execution risk on 14A, which needs customers to justify its capex.

Finance idea of the week: operating leverage, and why Intel's profit falls faster than its revenue

Intel's Q4 guide has revenue down about 6% and operating profit down about 54%. The concept behind this is operating leverage. When a large share of a company's costs is fixed, profit moves much more than revenue, both up and down.

A fixed cost stays the same in the short run no matter how much you sell. Intel's largest fixed costs are its fabs. A fab costs the same to depreciate, staff, power and maintain whether it runs at 90% or 60% of capacity. Intel recorded $8.9 billion of depreciation in just the first nine months of 2026. A variable cost rises and falls with volume. Raw silicon wafers and some packaging materials are examples.

Here are the numbers. In Q3 Intel had $16.5 billion of revenue and $7.28 billion of non-GAAP gross profit, a 44.1% margin. For Q4 it guides $15.5 billion of revenue at a 38% margin, or $5.89 billion of gross profit. Revenue falls about $1.0 billion and gross profit falls about $1.39 billion. Gross profit falls by more than revenue for three reasons:

  • Intel is cutting client wafer starts, so the same fixed fab costs are spread over fewer chips.
  • The product mix is less favorable.
  • The Fab 28 accident.

In Intel's words, the margin decline “primarily reflects lower overall revenue against our fixed-cost base as we adjust client wafer starts to current demand conditions.” Operating expenses (R&D and overhead, about $4.4 billion) are also mostly fixed in the short run. So nearly all of the $1.39 billion of lost gross profit comes straight out of operating income, which drops from about $3.2 billion to about $1.5 billion.

The same mechanism helped Intel in Q3. More server volume running through the same fabs lifted non-GAAP gross margin from 41.8% in Q2 to 44.1% in Q3, and non-GAAP EPS beat guidance by 14 cents. Operating leverage works in both directions. It is why Q3 beat and why Q4 disappointed.

The number to calculate is the decremental margin: the change in profit divided by the change in revenue. From Q3 to Q4 at the gross profit line it is about 1.39 / 1.01, roughly 137%. A company whose costs are mostly variable might lose 40 to 50 cents of gross profit for each dollar of lost revenue. A fab owner that is cutting production can lose more than a dollar, because producing less also raises the cost of each chip it still makes.

This is where companies that own fabs differ from companies that do not. AMD and Nvidia buy finished wafers from TSMC, so more of their cost of goods is variable: if they sell fewer chips, they buy fewer wafers. TSMC carries the fixed cost and the risk of running below capacity. Intel carries both the risk of designing chips and the risk of running factories (opinion/inference: this is general industry structure, not from this week's sources). That is why Intel's margins swing more, and why its foundry strategy depends on outside customers. Wafers made for outside customers are how a fab owner keeps its factories full when its own products slow down.

The common beginner mistake is to forecast revenue and then apply last quarter's margin percentage to get profit. For a company with heavy fab costs, that understates profit in an upturn and overstates it in a downturn. A better model splits costs into a fixed amount plus a variable percentage of revenue, and lets the margin come out of the arithmetic. Model Intel's 2027 that way, with fixed costs rising as the “up strongly” capex turns into depreciation and with PC revenue recovering only in the second half. You will see why the market took almost a third off the stock in one day, even though the quarter Intel had just reported was its best in years.
